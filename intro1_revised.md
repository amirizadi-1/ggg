# From Reconstruction to Regeneration: Mesenchymal Stem Cells and Tissue Engineering Strategies for Post-Treatment Lung Tissue Restoration in Non-small-cell Lung Cancer (NSCLC)

## A. Revised Introduction

Radiotherapy is a central component of treatment for non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC), the histological group that accounts for most lung cancers (1). It can be combined with chemotherapy, and the two are often given together as concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) (2). Because the lung around the tumor lies within or near the radiation field, the treatment that controls the cancer can also injure the organ in which it arose. Radiation pneumonitis (RP), an early inflammatory reaction of irradiated lung tissue (2), reaches clinical significance in an estimated 15% to 40% of patients with NSCLC who receive CCRT (1). In some patients this inflammatory phase is followed by radiation-induced pulmonary fibrosis (RIPF), a chronic and largely irreversible scarring process reported in 5% to 50% of irradiated patients across studies (3). Together, these conditions constitute radiation-induced lung injury (RILI), the main pulmonary complication of thoracic radiotherapy and a recognized constraint on its delivery (4). Its consequences outlast the treatment course: a tumor may be controlled while the remaining lung is left inflamed or scarred, with reduced function (3, 4).

The progression from pneumonitis to fibrosis reflects a cascade involving several cell populations rather than a single lesion. Radiation damages alveolar epithelial cells and vascular endothelial cells, and the ensuing response engages macrophages, fibroblasts and myofibroblasts through pathways that include transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) signaling and the high-mobility group box 1/nuclear factor-κB (HMGB1/NF-κB) axis (4). Once fibroblast activation and matrix deposition dominate, the injury is no longer purely inflammatory, and fibrotic tissue progressively replaces the alveolar structure that supports gas exchange (3, 4). Clinical management has largely followed this sequence instead of anticipating it. Treatment usually begins when symptoms of pneumonitis or fibrosis appear, and the options for established fibrosis remain mainly supportive (3). Drugs aimed at the underlying pathways have been proposed, yet most candidates described to date have been studied in preclinical models (4). Suppressing inflammation can relieve pneumonitis, but it does not rebuild the epithelial and endothelial compartments that radiation has destroyed. Not every irradiated patient develops lasting damage. For those who do, the unmet need is repair: limiting fibrotic remodeling and supporting recovery of the alveolar–capillary unit, not only controlling symptoms.

Mesenchymal stem/stromal cells (MSCs) have been investigated as one answer to this repair problem in the injured lung (5). They are adherent, multipotent cells that can be isolated from several tissues, including perinatal sources such as the umbilical cord, placenta and amniotic fluid (6), and adult human bone marrow-derived MSCs were shown to differentiate along several mesenchymal lineages in culture (7). Their potential value in RILI depends less on this lineage plasticity than on how they act within injured tissue. MSCs have been reported to migrate toward damaged sites and to support remodeling there (5), a property of interest because radiation injury is largely confined to the irradiated volume. They also modulate immune responses, mainly through secreted mediators, which bears directly on the inflammatory phase that precedes fibrosis. Much of the evidence for these effects, however, comes from other settings. In a mouse model of carbon tetrachloride-induced acute liver injury, for example, MSCs altered the metabolic profile of liver-resident immune cells (8), and the clinical studies summarized for injured lung concern acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and COVID-19 (6). Such data make a mechanism in irradiated lung plausible without demonstrating it. Direct replacement of alveolar epithelial or endothelial cells by transplanted MSCs is a still weaker basis for the argument, since differentiation in culture (7) does not show that these cells engraft and function as lung epithelium in vivo. For post-treatment lung injury, the case for MSCs therefore rests mainly on paracrine and immunomodulatory activity, with cell replacement remaining a hypothesis.

For any of these mechanisms to matter, MSCs must reach the injured region, remain there and stay functional, and each of these steps limits current cell-based therapies (9). After intravenous infusion, a large proportion of MSCs is retained in the lungs. Intravital imaging in mice showed that the cells arrested mechanically at branch points of the pulmonary microvasculature, where their size prevented further passage, and that retention increased during *Escherichia coli* infection (10). For a lung target, this first-pass entrapment is a mixed outcome. It brings cells to the organ but places them in capillaries rather than in injured alveolar tissue, and it was characterized in bacterial infection, not in irradiated lung. Survival after delivery is a second obstacle. In acute kidney injury, 80% to 90% of transplanted MSCs were reported to die within the first week, and the hypoxic, inflammatory environment of injured tissue also impaired the paracrine function on which their benefit depends (11). Variation in the quality of MSC preparations between tissue sources adds further uncertainty (9). Taken together, these observations suggest that infused MSCs may act only briefly and inconsistently. Cells that lodge in the vasculature or die within days are unlikely to sustain the signaling that durable repair of irradiated lung would require.

These constraints concern the environment into which cells are delivered as much as the cells themselves, and this is where tissue engineering enters the argument. Lung bioengineering now includes artificial scaffolds, other biomaterial-based systems and acellular lung matrices, which are being explored as supports for cell survival and integration (12). In this approach, MSCs are seeded within a three-dimensional scaffold that mimics features of the native extracellular matrix (ECM) and provides structural and biochemical support (13). Each feature of the design answers a specific weakness of infusion. Local implantation removes the dependence on passage through the pulmonary microvasculature, an ECM-like substrate gives adherent cells a surface to attach to, and a defined niche may help preserve paracrine output for longer. A study of umbilical cord-derived MSCs (UC-MSCs) on a biomimetic scaffold illustrates what this can achieve experimentally. In rats after partial lung resection, the scaffold-borne cells exerted strong paracrine effects; some migrated into the lung and showed long-term retention, inflammation and fibrosis in the residual lung were reduced, and vascular endothelial cells and alveolar type II epithelial cells entered the scaffold (13). These findings indicate better retention and recruitment of host cells. They were obtained after surgical tissue loss, however, rather than radiation injury, in which the surrounding parenchyma is itself inflamed or fibrotic, and host-cell ingrowth into a scaffold is not equivalent to regeneration of gas-exchanging alveoli. It is useful to keep three aims apart: using a scaffold to deliver and sustain MSCs, reconstructing lung structure, and regenerating tissue that functions. The evidence discussed here bears mainly on the first two.

The relevant evidence thus comes from lines of research that have developed largely in parallel. Studies of RILI define its mechanisms and clinical course (2–4). Evidence for MSC activity derives mostly from infection, ARDS and injuries of other organs (6, 8, 10, 11), and scaffold-based MSC delivery has been tested in a surgical resection model (13). None of these sources shows durable functional restoration of irradiated lung in patients treated for NSCLC. Several questions follow. It remains unclear how far results from infection, kidney, liver or resection models apply to lung that has been irradiated and may already be fibrotic, whether reductions in inflammation, fibrosis or histological damage translate into measurable gains in pulmonary function, and how long any benefit lasts. Because the recipients are patients treated for cancer, the oncological safety of introducing MSCs, alone or on scaffolds, into previously irradiated lung has to be weighed alongside efficacy.

This review addresses that gap. Taking RILI as the form of post-treatment lung injury most directly represented in the available evidence, we first outline how pneumonitis progresses to fibrosis and why current management leaves structural damage largely unrepaired. We then assess the mechanisms proposed for MSC-mediated repair, separating paracrine and immunomodulatory effects from the weaker case for cell replacement, and examine the delivery and survival problems that limit MSC infusion. Finally, we evaluate scaffold- and matrix-based strategies for MSC delivery, asking which of these limitations they can overcome and what evidence, including oncological safety data, would be needed to show functional regeneration rather than structural reconstruction of lung tissue in patients after NSCLC treatment.

## B. Major Changes Made

- **Built a single argument instead of a list of facts.** The original moved from epidemiology to MSC biology to scaffolds without explaining why each step followed. The revision follows one chain: radiotherapy for NSCLC → RP and RIPF (RILI) → why symptom control is not tissue repair → why MSCs are a candidate → why infused MSCs underperform → how scaffolds answer those specific failures → what remains unproven → the objective.
- **Added the missing paragraph on mechanism and management (paragraph 2).** The original jumped from incidence figures straight to “repairing RILI is a fundamental problem.” The new paragraph explains the epithelial/endothelial → inflammatory → fibrotic cascade (ref. 4) and why supportive care leaves structure unrepaired (refs. 3, 4), so that the need for repair is derived rather than asserted.
- **Reframed the MSC paragraph as a rationale, not an inventory.** Each property (homing, immunomodulation, differentiation) is now tied to a feature of RILI, and the paragraph states openly that the supporting data come from liver injury, ARDS and COVID-19. Differentiation into alveolar cells is demoted from an established property to a hypothesis, because ref. 7 shows only in vitro mesenchymal differentiation.
- **Corrected the logic of the “pulmonary trapping” argument.** The original treated lung trapping as preventing MSCs from reaching their target, which is contradictory when the target is the lung. The revision explains that trapping delivers cells to the lung but leaves them in capillaries rather than injured alveoli, and that this was shown in an infection model (ref. 10).
- **Made tissue engineering a response to named problems.** The original opened with an ungrammatical transition (“Not encouraging results from cell therapies’ models…”). Paragraph 5 now maps each scaffold feature to a limitation from paragraph 4 (vascular trapping, loss of anchorage, short-lived secretion) and separates delivery, structural reconstruction and functional regeneration.
- **Put the key experimental study in its real context.** Ref. 13 is now described as a rat partial lung resection model, not radiation injury, and host-cell ingrowth is distinguished from regenerated alveoli.
- **Added a defensible gap and a matching objective.** The original had no explicit gap, and its aim (“moving beyond simple reconstruction toward a truly regenerated lung”) was promotional. Paragraph 6 derives a narrow gap from the cited sources only, and paragraph 7 states a three-part scope that answers it, including oncological safety.
- **Reduced unhelpful statistics.** “Over 2 million cases,” “about 80%,” and “31.4% prevalence” were removed; RIPF incidence was corrected from “up to half” to the 5%–50% range given in ref. 3 (see Section C).
- **Language.** Fixed abbreviation errors (e.g., “radiation-induced pulmonary fibrosis)RIPF)”), capitalization, spacing and grammar; defined every abbreviation once; removed filler such as “unfortunately” and “somehow.”
- **Citation numbering kept.** All 13 original reference numbers are retained with their original meaning, and in the revised text they still appear in first-citation order (1 → 13), so the reference list needs no renumbering.

## D. Final Coherence Check

The revised Introduction follows the required chain: NSCLC and its radiotherapy (paragraph 1) → RILI and its consequences, with the repair need derived from the mechanism and the limits of supportive care (paragraphs 1–2) → the MSC rationale (paragraph 3) → MSC delivery and survival limits (paragraph 4) → tissue engineering as a response to those named limits (paragraph 5) → a narrow, source-based gap (paragraph 6) → an objective that answers it (paragraph 7).

The main caveat is evidential rather than structural: most mechanistic support for MSCs and scaffolds comes from liver, kidney, infection and lung-resection models, and the text says so instead of hiding it. Several figures and attributions (refs. 1, 2, 5, 6, 9, 11, 12) remain provisional until checked against the full texts, and the oncological-safety point needs new references in the body of the review.
