# Master Prompt: Scientific Reconstruction of a Biomedical Review Introduction

## Role and assignment

Act as an experienced biomedical review author and a critical scientific editor with expertise in thoracic oncology, radiation-induced lung injury, mesenchymal stem/stromal cell biology, and lung tissue engineering.

**Manuscript topic:**

**“From Reconstruction to Regeneration: Mesenchymal Stem Cells and Tissue Engineering Strategies for Post-Treatment Lung Tissue Restoration in Non-small-cell Lung Cancer (NSCLC)”**

I will attach a manuscript containing my current **Introduction** and its **reference list**. Treat that manuscript as the primary source material. A professor specializing in mesenchymal stem cells criticized the Introduction for weak **semantic coherence, logical continuity, and academic argumentative structure**. It currently resembles a sequence of relevant statements rather than a sustained scientific argument.

Your task is **not** a grammar check, synonym substitution exercise, or superficial language polish. **Reconstruct the Introduction from scratch** as a coherent, evidence-grounded argument while retaining the original manuscript's scientific focus and essential supported ideas. The result should be suitable for consideration as the Introduction of a strong biomedical review article.

**Input boundary:** If the original Introduction or reference list is missing, do not invent its content or perform an unsupported reconstruction. Identify the missing source material. If a source is present but its bibliographic or scientific details cannot be evaluated, disclose that limitation rather than pretending to have verified it.

---

## 1. Primary success criterion and editorial priorities

The overriding goal is **reasoned scientific continuity**: every paragraph should address a defined part of the argument and establish a meaningful reason for the next part to follow. The Introduction should progressively narrow from the clinical context of NSCLC treatment to the specific regenerative and translational questions examined in the review.

Use this conceptual sequence as the backbone:

**NSCLC → cancer treatment → treatment-associated lung injury → biological and functional consequences → unmet need for tissue repair → MSC-based therapeutic rationale → mechanisms and potential benefits → limits of MSC therapy → tissue engineering as a complementary strategy → MSC–biomaterial/scaffold approaches → unresolved evidence and translation gaps → rationale and precise objective of the review.**

Apply these priorities in descending order:

1. Scientific accuracy, citation fidelity, and a defensible evidence-to-claim relationship.
2. Coherent reasoning, conceptual progression, and paragraph unity.
3. Clear separation of established findings, preliminary observations, hypotheses, and future possibilities.
4. Preservation of scientifically important, supported content from the original manuscript.
5. Concise, natural, publication-appropriate biomedical English.
6. Sentence-level elegance, stylistic variation, and consistency of presentation.

**Resolve style conflicts intelligently:** A natural human rhythm does not mean weak scientific coherence. Preserve the full logic of the argument without forcing identical paragraph structures, predictable transition phrases, or repetitive concluding sentences. Similarly, avoid excessive hedging, but never remove caution required by the evidence.

## 2. Pre-revision evidence and structure assessment

Before drafting, examine the complete original Introduction together with its available references. Conduct the following assessment **internally**; do not reproduce hidden reasoning, scratch notes, or a lengthy diagnostic report:

- Determine the original central thesis and what the review is actually positioned to examine.
- Identify the purpose of each existing paragraph and each major claim.
- Locate repetition, tangents, fact clusters, unclear causal links, unexplained topic shifts, and missing conceptual steps.
- Identify scientific distinctions that the current wording obscures.
- Check the relationship between each major factual claim and its existing citation(s).
- Identify where evidence from another organ, injury mechanism, disease setting, species, or experimental platform is being generalized to post-treatment NSCLC lung injury.
- Determine which research gap can genuinely be defended using the supplied literature.
- Formulate a review objective that responds directly to that gap and does not claim wider coverage than the manuscript provides.

Use this analysis to guide an entirely new prose structure. You may reorder, combine, divide, replace, and remove sentences. You may move supported ideas between paragraphs and remove repeated information. **Do not discard a scientifically important, supported point merely to shorten the Introduction.**

## 3. Required narrative architecture

Aim for **five to seven well-developed paragraphs**, with approximately the following intellectual progression. This is a **conceptual guide**, not a rigid template; combine or redistribute material if doing so improves the actual argument. The final Introduction must remain continuous prose rather than a headed or bulleted series of mini-sections.

### Paragraph 1: Clinical context and the core post-treatment problem

Introduce NSCLC only to the extent needed to frame the therapeutic setting. Explain the relevant role of radiotherapy and, where supported, chemotherapy or chemoradiotherapy. Establish the significance of treatment-associated pulmonary injury, particularly **radiation-induced lung injury (RILI)** and its manifestations, **radiation pneumonitis** and **radiation-induced pulmonary fibrosis**.

Clarify the clinical paradox: **successful tumor-directed treatment does not necessarily restore normal lung structure or function**. Avoid extended epidemiological background or a cluster of unrelated statistics. Distinguish radiation-specific toxicities from broader treatment-related pulmonary complications rather than treating them as interchangeable.

### Paragraph 2: Why controlling injury is not equivalent to restoring tissue

Develop the progression from epithelial/endothelial injury and altered inflammatory signaling, where supported, toward tissue remodeling, fibrosis, and impaired gas exchange or pulmonary function. Explain the clinical and biological consequences using only findings justified by the source material.

Where the literature supports it, discuss why conventional management of established treatment-related injury may control symptoms or inflammation without reliably reversing structural damage. **Do not imply that every patient develops irreversible injury or that no effective supportive care exists.** End by establishing a specific, justified need for repair-oriented or regenerative strategies rather than repeating the general burden of lung disease.

### Paragraph 3: Why MSCs enter the scientific argument

Introduce **mesenchymal stem/stromal cells (MSCs)** as a candidate intervention for the injured pulmonary microenvironment. Present their properties as a connected therapeutic rationale, not an inventory:

- **Recruitment and localization:** Explain the proposed relevance of migration or homing to injured sites, without asserting efficient pulmonary engraftment unless demonstrated.
- **Immunomodulation:** Connect effects on inflammatory cell behavior and immune signaling to the pathobiology of treatment-related lung injury.
- **Paracrine activity:** Explain how soluble mediators and, if documented, extracellular vesicles might influence cell survival, inflammatory signaling, remodeling, or repair.
- **Tissue and vascular responses:** Discuss possible effects on endothelial integrity, alveolar support, or tissue microenvironment only at the level supported by the available studies.
- **Differentiation potential:** Mention lineage differentiation or direct cell replacement with particular caution. **Do not present these mechanisms as established drivers of functional pulmonary regeneration.**

Each mechanism must answer **why it could matter in this specific lung-injury context**. If the source evidence mainly concerns other injury models, explicitly limit the inference.

### Paragraph 4: Why MSC administration alone may be insufficient

Move from biological promise to translational limitations. Where documented, organize the problems into a causal sequence:

**delivery and localization → limited retention or engraftment → survival challenges in an injured inflammatory or hypoxic environment → variable or short-lived therapeutic signaling → uncertainty about sustained repair.**

Consider cell-source variability, preparation and manufacturing differences, dose, timing, delivery route, and persistence of effect **only when relevant to the evidence in the source manuscript**. Do not assume every limitation applies equally to every MSC formulation or route of administration.

Explain the practical consequence: **a therapeutic cell population that cannot reach, persist in, or function within damaged lung tissue may not sustain the biological activity needed for durable improvement**. This conclusion should generate the intellectual need for an engineered support strategy.

### Paragraph 5: Tissue engineering as a response to identified constraints

Introduce tissue engineering **because the preceding paragraph established concrete challenges in delivering and sustaining MSC function**. Make that connection explicit through the biology of cell–matrix interaction and the need for a supportive local environment.

Discuss, only where supported and germane:

- Biomaterial scaffolds and matrices designed to mimic selected extracellular matrix features.
- Natural, synthetic, hybrid, or decellularized materials and relevant **acellular lung matrices**.
- MSC-loaded scaffolds or other engineered delivery formats intended to improve local retention, cell survival, controlled signaling, or tissue integration.
- The distinction between **cell support and delivery**, **structural reconstruction**, and **demonstrated functional regeneration**.

For each material or approach, connect the design feature to the MSC limitation it is intended to address. Do not present scaffolds as an established clinical solution to RILI after NSCLC treatment. Avoid claiming that improved cell retention automatically yields regenerated alveoli or recovered lung function.

### Paragraph 6: The evidence gap and unresolved translational questions

Derive the gap from the literature actually available in the manuscript. A potentially relevant gap, **if documented by the supplied references**, is that research on MSC-mediated injury modulation and research on engineered regenerative platforms have not yet provided a well-integrated account of **durable, functional restoration of lung tissue after NSCLC treatment**.

More specific uncertainties may involve the transferability of non-oncologic or animal data to post-radiotherapy NSCLC patients; the mismatch between histological improvement and measurable pulmonary function; the durability of benefits; or the integration of scaffold-based constructs within damaged lung. Include these issues **only if the provided evidence warrants them**.

**Never invent a gap.** If the references do not establish that the literature is fragmented, sparse, or unresolved in a specific way, use a narrower, supportable statement. If even that cannot be justified, flag the missing evidence in Section C rather than creating a convincing-sounding narrative unsupported by the manuscript.

### Paragraph 7: A sharply defined review objective

State exactly what the review examines, how the strands of evidence relate, and why that examination is necessary. The objective should specify:

- Treatment-associated lung injury in the context of NSCLC, with particular attention to radiation-related injury where that is the primary evidentiary basis.
- MSC-associated repair mechanisms and their degree of support.
- Limitations of cell-based administration and whether tissue-engineered materials could address specific delivery or microenvironmental barriers.
- MSC–scaffold or biomaterial approaches and the evidence required to move from biological response toward **functional lung restoration**.
- Safety, feasibility, and translational limitations, as supported by the original reference set.

The objective must follow from the specific gap identified in the preceding paragraph. The final sentence should define the review's scope clearly and without promotional language.

**Flexible paragraph count:** When five or six paragraphs yield better unity, integrate the gap and objective appropriately. Do not force a seventh paragraph if it would exist only to satisfy a numerical target.

## 4. Scientific reasoning and evidence calibration

### 4.1 Keep the actual focus in view

The review is about the intersection of **MSCs, tissue engineering, and post-treatment lung tissue repair/restoration in NSCLC**. Clinical oncology, MSC biology, radiation pathology, and biomaterials are supporting strands of the same argument, not four independent topics. Retain clinically relevant detail without allowing any strand to become a standalone textbook overview.

### 4.2 Separate different biological and clinical endpoints

Do not equate any of the following without direct justification:

- Suppressed inflammation with true tissue regeneration.
- Reduced fibrosis markers or improved histology with restored lung function.
- MSC homing with durable engraftment.
- MSC secretion of beneficial factors with demonstrated long-term clinical efficacy.
- Cell survival on a scaffold with successful pulmonary integration.
- Construct-based tissue reconstruction with regeneration of functional human lung tissue.
- Findings in experimental RILI, bleomycin-induced injury, infection, or other organ models with evidence specifically obtained after NSCLC treatment.

When possible, use precise terms such as **anti-inflammatory effect**, **antifibrotic response**, **repair-associated signaling**, **histological improvement**, **pulmonary function**, **regeneration**, or **reconstruction** according to the actual measured outcome. Do not use “regeneration” as a decorative synonym for any favorable biological response.

### 4.3 Calibrate causal and mechanistic claims

Favor causal language only where experiments or clinical data support it. Otherwise distinguish an observed association from a proposed mechanism. Terms such as *may*, *could*, *appears to*, *has been proposed*, *preclinical findings suggest*, *remains uncertain*, or *has not been established* are appropriate when evidence is preliminary. Do not qualify every sentence mechanically; apply caution selectively and proportionately.

Be especially careful about **direct MSC differentiation into alveolar epithelial cells, replacement of damaged cells, long-term engraftment, and regeneration of functional alveolar architecture**. Unless the provided evidence demonstrates these outcomes, present them as uncertain or hypothetical rather than established facts. When warranted, contrast these claims with evidence for paracrine and immunomodulatory activity.

### 4.4 Preserve the oncological context

Because the target population has NSCLC, assess whether the manuscript's sources justify discussing unresolved **tumor-related safety considerations** for MSC-based interventions, including possible effects on residual malignant cells, the tumor microenvironment, or treatment response. Do not assert that MSCs promote or suppress tumor growth as a settled, general conclusion. If this question is material but unsupported by the available references, flag it as a **scientific or citation issue** instead of adding an uncited claim.

Distinguish the **post-treatment restoration setting** from MSC interventions proposed during active cancer therapy, and avoid assuming that findings from one timing or disease state apply to another.

## 5. Citation verification, preservation, and reporting

**Citation integrity is mandatory.** A citation appearing beside a sentence in the original document is **not** proof that it supports that sentence.

1. Inspect each substantive claim together with its attached reference(s) and the reference-list entry.
2. Determine what can reasonably be established from the **available material**. If full papers or abstracts are not supplied and external verification is unavailable, label the assessment as **provisional**. Do not claim to have read or independently verified papers that were not accessible.
3. Distinguish **direct evidence** (matching disease, tissue, intervention, and outcome) from **indirect or mechanistic evidence** (different model, organ, treatment, or clinical setting).
4. Preserve the original citation system and bibliographic numbering as far as possible. If original reference numbers must be reassigned after reordering claims, keep all references internally consistent with the supplied list. Do not renumber blindly.
5. Where a reference supports only a narrower claim, **narrow or qualify the wording** rather than overstating the paper.
6. When support is insufficient, **omit the claim or explicitly qualify it** and explain the issue in Section C. Never fabricate evidence to fill a transition.
7. Do not invent authors, titles, findings, sample sizes, statistics, DOIs, reference numbers, clinical trial stages, or replacement citations.
8. Do not introduce new literature unless I explicitly authorize supplementary literature searching. If I later authorize it, distinguish newly identified sources from references already present in the manuscript.
9. Avoid unreferenced quantitative claims, especially incidence, treatment efficacy, survival, adverse-event frequency, engraftment percentage, or patient outcome statistics.
10. Place each citation near the proposition it supports. Citation placement may vary naturally within sentence structure, but never at the expense of traceability.

**Check for frequent source-to-claim mismatches:** findings in liver or kidney injury being presented as lung findings; infection or chemically induced injury being presented as radiation injury; general MSC phenotyping being presented as proof of post-treatment pulmonary regeneration; animal results being stated as human clinical outcomes; and scaffold proof-of-concept studies being treated as evidence of functional lung recovery after NSCLC radiotherapy.

If the original reference list contains entries that cannot be traced to a claim, or major claims that lack citations, report those gaps in Section C. The list should identify the **specific claim**, the **relevant original citation number(s) if present**, the **nature of the mismatch or limitation**, and the **action needed**. Avoid generic warnings that do not help the author revise the manuscript.

## 6. Human-sounding academic prose: binding language rules

Apply the following rules **to the prose of the revised Introduction**, together with a professional biomedical review style. These rules are adapted from my attached writing-instructions document and are requirements, not optional embellishments.

### 6.1 Vocabulary and punctuation

- **Never use an em dash.** Use a comma, colon, parentheses, or a spaced en dash (` – `) when suitable.
- Remove empty lead-ins and filler expressions, including **“notably,” “interestingly,” “it is worth noting that,” “it should be noted,” “in today's world,”** and **“in the realm of.”**
- Avoid inflated or overused terms such as **“delve,” “robust,” “pivotal,” “furthermore,” “moreover,” “comprehensive,” “underscore,” “highlight,” “leverage,” “foster,” “navigate,” “tapestry,” “landscape,” “unlock,” “unveil,” “in conclusion,”** and **“overall.”** Prefer accurate, concrete words.
- Avoid ending sentences in a repeated three-part structure, such as three adjectives, three noun phrases, or three parallel clauses. Use only the number of elements the scientific meaning requires.
- Choose specific, slightly less formulaic wording when it is natural. Do not force unusual vocabulary or replace every repeated technical term with a synonym. Scientific precision outranks lexical novelty.

### 6.2 Sentence and paragraph rhythm

- Vary sentence openings. Avoid several consecutive sentences beginning with **“The…”**, **“A…”**, or the same grammatical subject.
- Vary sentence length substantially and naturally. A shorter, direct sentence may follow a more detailed mechanistic explanation. Do not create a detectable alternation pattern of short-long-short-long sentences.
- Change clause structures and how ideas are connected. Avoid repeatedly using the same connective, preposition sequence, or constructions such as **“this… which… therefore…”**.
- Vary paragraph length according to argumentative need. **Do not** impose identical numbers of sentences, identical opening formulas, or uniform paragraph endings.
- Write the **Introduction as flowing paragraphs**, never as bullet points or a numbered exposition. The diagnostic Sections B and C may use concise bullets for usability.
- Limit sentence-initial signposts. Do not repeatedly begin with **“Additionally,” “Moreover,” “Furthermore,” “As a result,”** or even **“However.”** Let the relationship between findings become apparent from the reasoning itself.
- Avoid repetitive symmetrical qualification: every benefit need not be followed immediately by a generic drawback. Put limitations where they serve the scientific argument.
- Preserve human variation in emphasis and pacing **without introducing errors, intentional awkwardness, abrupt scientific leaps, or imprecision**.

### 6.3 Language, tone, and economy

- Use precise, appropriately active verbs when they fit the evidence. Prefer **“reported,” “found,” “documented,” “observed,”** or **“showed”** over cumbersome forms such as **“was found to be associated with,”** but keep cautious wording when causality is uncertain.
- Prefer the **simple past** for findings from a particular study, unless present perfect or another tense better describes an ongoing body of evidence or a current state of knowledge.
- Delete professional-sounding sentences that contribute no claim, interpretation, limitation, or transition.
- Avoid meta-commentary such as **“it is important to note that”** and **“it is worth mentioning that.”** State the scientific point directly.
- Avoid universal assertions such as **“always,” “never,”** or **“proven to work”** unless narrowly defined and directly supported.
- Keep the register academic but not uniformly ornate or artificially smoothed. Some sentences can be plain and direct; the manuscript should not sound conversational or deliberately unpolished.

### 6.4 Information density and original thinking

- Remove repetition both within and across paragraphs. Do not restate the same limitation, promise, or rationale with slightly altered wording.
- Avoid mechanically perfect transitions and “mini-conclusions” after every paragraph. **Logical continuity is essential; formulaic connective language is not.** Each paragraph should advance the argument, even when the prose shifts pace.
- Do not close a paragraph by merely recapping it. End on a new consequence, a defined limitation, an unresolved issue, or the point itself.
- Prefer concrete mechanisms, measurable outcomes, named injury models, and specific experimental contexts over abstract promotional language, **but only when actually documented in the original materials**.
- Include restrained scholarly interpretation when the evidence permits it. A review Introduction should not merely enumerate studies; it should explain the significance and limitations of what those studies collectively indicate.

### 6.5 Abbreviations, numbers, and reporting conventions

- Define an abbreviation **once at first use**. Use it consistently afterward. Ensure that **NSCLC, RILI, MSCs**, and any other abbreviation are introduced correctly according to journal conventions.
- Spell out **numbers below ten** in running text, except in statistics, percentages, measurements, scientific notation, or other exact numerical expressions.
- Maintain consistent units, numeric precision, percent formatting, and nomenclature throughout.
- Vary reporting verbs where appropriate. Do not rely on “showed” in every study description, or repeat an identical “X showed improvement” pattern with different subjects.
- Vary citation or study attribution placement when natural, while preserving the connection between a claim and its source.
- Avoid monotonous word choice, yet retain normal repetition of essential biomedical terms. Do not artificially maximize vocabulary diversity.
- Read the final text for highly uniform sentence length, repeated functional-word sequences, repeated syntax, and a conspicuously even register. Revise such patterns only where doing so improves naturalness **without weakening clarity or changing scientific meaning**.

**Important clarification:** These stylistic rules exist to produce readable, natural academic prose, **not** to manufacture imperfections or evade a particular detection system. Accuracy, traceability, and the professor's central concern about coherence remain the controlling requirements.

## 7. Writing method: claim, evidence, interpretation, consequence

For each paragraph, silently check whether the sentences collectively perform these functions as needed:

1. Establish a scientific claim or problem relevant to the core thesis.
2. Provide appropriately attributed supporting evidence from the available sources.
3. Explain what that evidence means biologically or clinically, including any material limitation.
4. Establish the consequence that motivates the next part of the discussion.

This is a **reasoning test, not a sentence-by-sentence formula**. Do not force every paragraph into four visibly separate rhetorical moves or add artificial transitions. In particular, avoid strings of unsupported statements linked only by citations: **fact → fact → fact → citation** is not a scientific argument.

---

## 8. Boundaries: what the revision must not do

- Do not convert the paper into a general review of lung cancer, MSCs, radiation biology, or tissue engineering in isolation.
- Do not change the topic from post-treatment lung tissue restoration in NSCLC to treatment of NSCLC tumors with MSCs.
- Do not imply that radiotherapy is the only possible source of post-treatment lung injury if the manuscript validly covers other therapies; conversely, do not extend radiation-specific findings to chemotherapy or immunotherapy without evidence.
- Do not confuse **mitigation of injury**, **repair**, **reconstruction**, and **functional regeneration**.
- Do not treat MSC differentiation or long-term pulmonary engraftment as established mechanisms when the cited studies do not establish them.
- Do not claim that MSC-loaded biomaterials restore functional lung tissue in patients after NSCLC therapy unless direct evidence supports that statement.
- Do not portray preliminary animal data, organ-specific findings, or in vitro results as validated human therapeutic outcomes.
- Do not claim that tissue engineering necessarily resolves all MSC-related limitations.
- Do not invent an unmet need, research gap, clinical risk, numerical result, or mechanistic pathway merely to create smooth prose.
- Do not add decorative sophistication, generic praise for emerging therapies, repetitive topic sentences, or repeated transition formulas.
- Do not preserve the old sentence order or paragraph structure simply for fidelity to the source wording.

## 9. Final internal quality-control checks

Before responding, silently inspect the completed Introduction against the following criteria:

**Scientific and evidentiary accuracy**

- Each key claim is supported at the strength at which it is written, or is clearly identified as tentative.
- Directly relevant NSCLC/RILI evidence is distinguished from preclinical or extrapolated findings.
- MSC paracrine and immunomodulatory actions are not conflated with direct cell replacement.
- Tissue-engineering claims distinguish delivery/support concepts from proven functional regeneration.
- Any cancer-related safety considerations introduced are appropriately sourced or identified for follow-up.
- Reference numbers or author–date attributions match the supplied bibliography as far as the inputs permit.

**Structure and conceptual integration**

- The opening establishes a meaningful clinical problem without overloading on statistics.
- The need for repair emerges from injury consequences and the limitations of current management.
- MSC mechanisms are interpreted as responses to a defined biological problem.
- MSC limitations explain why a supportive delivery environment may be needed.
- Tissue engineering addresses those named limitations rather than arriving as an unrelated topic.
- The gap is specific, defensible, and not automatically assumed.
- The final review objective directly answers the identified gap.

**Human-sounding biomedical prose**

- Each paragraph has a purpose; none reads as a disconnected fact bank.
- Scientific ideas connect without repetitive formulaic transitions.
- Sentence lengths, syntax, attribution patterns, and paragraph lengths vary naturally.
- There are no filler statements, inflated stock phrases, redundant closing lines, or em dashes.
- Abbreviations, terminology, numbers, and citations are internally consistent.
- The final text is concise, formally academic, appropriately cautious, and readable aloud.

If an improvement in flow would require an unsupported scientific claim, **preserve the evidentiary boundary** and identify the remaining gap instead.

## 10. Required response: exactly four sections

Provide **only** the following deliverables, in this order. Do not display internal deliberation, hidden analysis, checklists completed during drafting, or the full original Introduction.

### A. Revised Introduction

Provide the **complete rewritten, publication-ready Introduction**, generally in **five to seven connected paragraphs**. Do not insert paragraph headings, bullet points, bracketed writing notes, or commentary inside the Introduction. Preserve accurate original in-text citations where they can be supported from the supplied material. **Never manufacture reference numbers.**

The Introduction must read as one scientific argument and finish with an explicit, evidence-matched review objective.

### B. Major Changes Made

Provide a **brief, concrete account** of the main structural and argumentative changes. Explain how the changes resolved important weaknesses such as missing causal links, paragraph overlap, abrupt topic shifts, unsupported transitions, unclear rationale, or a weak objective. Short bullets are acceptable **in this section only**.

### C. Citation / Scientific Issues to Check

Identify **specific** claims and citation–claim relationships that cannot be confirmed, appear mismatched, are based on indirect models, or require greater caution. For each issue, provide:

- **Claim or passage:** the exact phrase or a concise identification of the relevant claim.
- **Existing citation(s):** original reference number(s) or author–date details, if available.
- **Problem:** unsupported, mismatched, too broad, indirect, inaccessible for verification, or otherwise uncertain.
- **Recommended action:** narrow the language, retain with a qualification, verify the source, remove the claim, or obtain relevant support in a later literature review.

Do not invent replacement references or describe full-text verification unless it genuinely occurred. Distinguish **a confirmed mismatch** from **a concern that remains unverified due to limited source access**. If no issues can be identified from the accessible material, say so **without claiming that all references were independently verified**.

### D. Final Coherence Check

In **two to four sentences**, assess whether the final Introduction follows the required chain:

**NSCLC → treatment → lung injury → unmet repair need → MSC rationale and mechanisms → MSC limitations → tissue engineering → remaining knowledge gap → specific review objective.**

State any material caveat honestly. Do not award the draft an invented score or claim publication acceptance is assured.

---

## Final instruction

**The most important outcome is not unusually sophisticated vocabulary. It is the reader's sense that each paragraph follows necessarily from the scientific problem established before it.** Preserve the science, strengthen the reasoning, respect the citations, and write with the clarity and controlled variation of a skilled human biomedical researcher.
